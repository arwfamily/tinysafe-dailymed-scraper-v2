How the Check works.

We don't score or rank sunscreens. We read every label the FDA has on file, the printed Drug Facts and the filing behind it, and show what we find as facts, each with its official source. What to avoid is your call. The rules are the same for every brand, including ours.

Sunnytime is checked the same way. I make a mineral sunscreen. When ours is on the FDA registry it goes through these exact rules, and what they find about it shows up like everyone else's, including the synthetic polymer powders it uses for texture.

How a result is laid out

  • At a glance. The same checks on every product, in the same order, so two sunscreens can be compared side by side: FDA recall, Broad Spectrum, the UV filters (mineral or chemical), spray or powder, an age limit or adult skincare active, fragrance, and a possible SPF booster listed as an inactive ingredient.
  • About the UV filters. Each chemical filter on the label, with its official limits in the US and the EU, and where state or county law bans its sale.
  • Label check. What the label itself shows: a UV filter listed as an inactive, a fragrance-free claim next to a fragrance, an ingredient banned in EU cosmetics.
  • Check for your child. Ingredients that matter mainly for some children. Tap Eczema, Psoriasis, Food allergy or Under 3 and the ones that apply are highlighted, with the guidance written for that situation.
  • Other ingredients some parents ask about. Ingredients parents often ask about, with the official findings we found for each. Listed because parents ask, not because a rule flags them.

What the colours mean:

  • Green: the check found nothing of that kind on the label.
  • Amber: pediatric or dermatology guidance (AAP, AAD) applies, the product is above an EU limit, an EU rule restricts the ingredient for young children, or the item matches what you told us about your child.
  • Blue: a fact to know: where a law bans its sale, how a rule elsewhere treats it, a study finding, or something the label leaves unclear.
  • Red: an FDA recall, an FDA order or warning that removes the ingredient or product type, or a combination pediatricians advise against.

Products are never ranked by how many items they have. Under every item, "Why this matters" lists the facts behind it, one sentence per source, then what points the other way and what no source has settled.

Every fact, with its source

These are the same sentences the result card shows. A law is called a law, a proposal a proposal, a recommendation a recommendation, and a single study a study.

At a glance

FDA recall
  • FDA Class I: “a reasonable probability” that using the product “will cause serious adverse health consequences or death.” FDA · checked 2026-10-26 agency guidance
  • FDA Class II: the product “may cause temporary or medically reversible adverse health consequences,” or serious harm is remote. FDA · checked 2026-10-26 agency guidance
  • FDA Class III: using the product “is not likely to cause adverse health consequences.” FDA · checked 2026-10-26 agency guidance
  • Each FDA recall record names the firm that recalled and which products, sizes, lots or stores it covers. FDA · checked weekly federal database

Not known

  • A recall on record does not mean the bottle you have was affected: check the lot, size or store in the record.
Benzene recalls
  • Aerosol spray sunscreens are among the products FDA lists as recalled for benzene contamination. FDA · current as of 2025-03-12 agency guidance
Broad Spectrum and SPF
  • Under FDA labeling rules, “Broad Spectrum” means the product passed a test for UVA protection in addition to UVB. FDA · 2011 rule federal regulation
  • Sunscreens that are not Broad Spectrum, or are below SPF 15, must carry the warning: “This product has been shown only to help prevent sunburn, not skin cancer or early skin aging.” FDA · updated 2026-10-16 agency guidance
  • FDA: choose a broad-spectrum sunscreen with an SPF of at least 15. FDA · updated 2026-10-16 agency guidance
  • The CDC: SPF 15 or higher, broad spectrum, every 2 hours. CDC · undated agency guidance
  • The AAP: at least SPF 15 “(up to SPF 50)”; “an SPF of 15 or 30 should be fine for most people.” American Academy of Pediatrics · updated 2024-08-09 medical society guidance
  • The American Academy of Dermatology recommends SPF 30 or higher. American Academy of Dermatology · updated 2025-02-11 medical society guidance
  • Health Canada recommends SPF 30 or higher. Health Canada · 2018-06-01 agency guidance
  • FDA has proposed (not finalized) that the maximum labeled SPF be 60+. FDA · 2021-09-24 proposed order (not final)
Mineral or chemical UV filters
  • The AAP's environmental health page says: “Mineral-based sunscreen (e.g., zinc oxide) is preferable to chemical-based sunscreen (e.g., oxybenzone).” American Academy of Pediatrics · updated 2024-01-10 medical society guidance
  • The AAP's 2021 safety tips say: “If possible, choose a sunscreen with the mineral ingredients zinc oxide or titanium dioxide.” American Academy of Pediatrics · 2021-06-07 medical society guidance
  • In 2021 FDA proposed (still not final) that zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are generally recognized as safe and effective at up to 25%. FDA · 2021-09-24 proposed order (not final)
  • For 12 older chemical filters, FDA proposed in 2021 that more safety data are needed; FDA says this is not a conclusion that they are unsafe. FDA · current as of 2026-09-10 agency guidance
  • In an FDA study in healthy adults who applied sunscreen to most of the body, six chemical filters (avobenzone, oxybenzone, octocrylene, homosalate, octisalate, octinoxate) reached blood levels above the point at which FDA asks for further safety studies, after one application. The authors said this does not mean people should stop using sunscreen. JAMA (study by FDA scientists) · 2020-01-21 peer-reviewed study (adults)

What points the other way

  • The AAD: “the science doesn't show that any sunscreen ingredients currently available in the U.S. are harmful to human health.” American Academy of Dermatology · updated 2025-02-11 medical society guidance
  • FDA: “The fact that an ingredient is absorbed through the skin and into the body does not mean the ingredient is unsafe.” FDA · 2019 agency commentary
  • The AAD says any of these sunscreens can protect you if it is broad spectrum, water resistant and SPF 30 or higher. American Academy of Dermatology · updated 2025-02-11 medical society guidance
  • The AAP: “using any sunscreen is better than not using sunscreen at all.” American Academy of Pediatrics · updated 2024-08-09 medical society guidance

Not known

  • FDA's absorption studies were done in adults; we found no comparable study in children.
Spray
  • FDA: spray sunscreens “should never be applied directly to your face.” FDA · updated 2026-10-16 agency guidance
  • The AAD advises never spraying around the face or mouth or into the wind, to avoid inhaling it. American Academy of Dermatology · updated 2025-02-11 medical society guidance
  • A pediatric dermatologist writing on the AAP's site says spray propellants “should not be inhaled by babies and children.” Dr. Sheilagh Maguiness, pediatric dermatologist, on the AAP's HealthyChildren.org · updated 2024-08-09 expert article on an AAP site
  • FDA lists aerosol sunscreens among products recalled for benzene contamination; FDA says benzene is not usually used to make drugs, so it appears as a contaminant. FDA · current as of 2025-03-12 agency guidance
  • In the EU, nano zinc oxide may not be used in applications that could expose the user's lungs by inhalation. European Union · 2016-04-21 EU law
  • The same EU condition applies to nano titanium dioxide. European Union · 2016-07-13 EU law

What points the other way

  • FDA's 2021 proposal would keep sprays as an allowed sunscreen form, subject to testing and labeling requirements. FDA · 2021-09-24 proposed order (not final)
Age limits
  • US sunscreen labeling rules require the directions to say “children under 6 months of age: Ask a doctor.” FDA · 2011 rule federal regulation
  • FDA: “Sunscreen is not recommended for infants under six months old.” FDA · updated 2026-10-16 agency guidance
  • FDA's infant page adds that “the best sun protection for these infants is to stay in the shade.” FDA · current as of 2026-09-10 agency guidance
  • The AAP allows sunscreen on small areas such as the face if clothing and shade are not available. American Academy of Pediatrics · updated 2024-08-09 medical society guidance
  • Health Canada: “Do not put sunscreen on babies less than 6 months of age.” Health Canada · 2018-06-01 agency guidance

FDA orders and pediatric guidance

Combined with an insect repellent
  • “AAP does not recommend products that combine sunscreen with DEET.” American Academy of Pediatrics · 2021-06-07 medical society guidance
  • The AAD does not recommend sunscreen with insect repellent either. American Academy of Dermatology · updated 2025-02-11 medical society guidance
  • Health Canada says sunscreen and insect repellent can be used safely together as separate products: apply the sunscreen first, then the repellent. Health Canada · 2018-06-01 agency guidance
PABA or trolamine salicylate
  • FDA issued a final order in September 2026 that sunscreens with PABA or trolamine salicylate as actives are not generally recognized as safe and effective; it takes effect in September 2027. FDA · issued 2026-09-11, effective 2027-09-11 final order
Hydroquinone
  • FDA: “there are no FDA-approved or otherwise legally marketed OTC skin lightening products.” FDA · 2022-04-19 agency notice
  • FDA says over-the-counter hydroquinone products are deemed new drugs and are misbranded, and had to come off the market by September 23, 2020 unless FDA approved them. FDA · 2022-04-19 agency notice
  • FDA has received reports of serious side effects, including skin rashes, facial swelling and ochronosis, a skin discoloration that may be permanent. FDA · 2022-04-19 agency notice

UV filters

Oxybenzone
  • The AAP: “If possible, avoid the sunscreen ingredient oxybenzone because of concerns about mild hormonal properties.” American Academy of Pediatrics · updated 2024-08-09 medical society guidance
  • The EU limits oxybenzone to 6% in face, hand and lip products (not sprays) and 2.2% in body products, including sprays. European Union · 2022-07-07 EU law
  • Hawaii has banned the sale of sunscreens containing oxybenzone (or octinoxate) without a prescription since January 1, 2021, citing harm to coral reefs. State of Hawaii · 2018-07-03, sale ban from 2021-01-01 state law

What points the other way

  • The EU scientific committee found oxybenzone safe within those limits (6% face, hand and lip; 2.2% body). EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety · 2021-03 EU scientific opinion
  • The AAD: “the science doesn't show that any sunscreen ingredients currently available in the U.S. are harmful to human health.” American Academy of Dermatology · updated 2025-02-11 medical society guidance
  • FDA: absorption into the body “does not mean the ingredient is unsafe.” FDA · 2019 agency commentary
Homosalate above the EU limit
  • The EU allows homosalate only in face products (not propellant sprays), up to 7.34%. European Union · 2022-11-10 EU law
  • That limit followed the EU scientific committee's opinion that homosalate is safe up to 7.34% in face cream and pump spray. EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety · 2021-12-02 EU scientific opinion
  • The US allows up to 15%. FDA · posted 2021-09-24 (FDA has amended the monograph since) federal monograph
  • Homosalate reached blood levels above FDA's further-testing threshold in an FDA study in healthy adults. JAMA (study by FDA scientists) · 2020-01-21 peer-reviewed study (adults)

What points the other way

  • FDA says its 2021 proposal that more data are needed for homosalate and other filters “does not represent a conclusion by the FDA” that they are unsafe. FDA · current as of 2026-09-10 agency guidance
Octocrylene
  • A 2021 study found benzophenone in the octocrylene-containing sunscreens and skin-care products it tested, and levels rose after an accelerated-aging test. Sorbonne Université (study published in Chem. Res. Toxicol.) · 2021-03-08 university press release on a peer-reviewed study
  • California lists benzophenone under Proposition 65 as a chemical known to cause cancer. California OEHHA · listed 2012-06-22 state law listing
  • EU law requires benzophenone from octocrylene to be kept at trace levels. European Union · 2022-07-07 EU law
  • In a 2025 safety review, Australia's TGA recommended limiting benzophenone in sunscreens to 383 ppm; this is a recommendation, not yet a final rule. Australia TGA · 2025-07 agency review

What points the other way

  • The EU scientific committee found octocrylene safe as a UV filter up to 10%. EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety · 2021-03 EU scientific opinion

Not known

  • We found no study measuring how much benzophenone a child's skin absorbs from an aged sunscreen, or whether that amount causes harm.

Laws on sale and SPF

Water resistance
  • FDA allows “Water Resistant (40 minutes)” or “(80 minutes)” only after a set water test. FDA · 2011 rule federal regulation
  • FDA: reapply at least every two hours, and more often if you're sweating or swimming. FDA · updated 2026-10-16 agency guidance
  • The AAP advises reapplying every 2 hours and after swimming, sweating or drying off with a towel. American Academy of Pediatrics · updated 2024-08-09 medical society guidance
Sale banned in Hawaii
  • Hawaii bans the sale of sunscreens containing oxybenzone or octinoxate without a prescription (Act 104, from January 1, 2021), citing harm to coral reefs. State of Hawaii · 2018-07-03, sale ban from 2021-01-01 state law
Sale banned on Maui
  • Maui County bans the sale, distribution and use of non-mineral sunscreens without a prescription, from October 1, 2022. Maui County · effective 2022-10-01 county law
SPF below 30
  • The American Academy of Dermatology recommends SPF 30 or higher. American Academy of Dermatology · updated 2025-02-11 medical society guidance
  • The AAP: at least SPF 15 “(up to SPF 50)”; “an SPF of 15 or 30 should be fine for most people.” American Academy of Pediatrics · updated 2024-08-09 medical society guidance
  • FDA: choose a broad-spectrum sunscreen with an SPF of at least 15. FDA · updated 2026-10-16 agency guidance
Legal limits for UV filters
  • FDA's sunscreen monograph lists the active ingredients (UV filters) an over-the-counter sunscreen may use, each with a maximum strength. FDA · posted 2021-09-24 (FDA has amended the monograph since) federal monograph
  • EU law lists the UV filters cosmetics may contain, each with a maximum concentration and any conditions of use. European Union · 2026-05-18 EU law
  • Australia lists the ingredients sunscreens may contain, with their limits. Australia TGA · commenced 2026-06-12 federal regulation

Not known

  • A limit in one country does not by itself show harm in another: each regulator sets limits on its own safety review.
SPF above 50
  • The AAP recommends SPF at least 15 “(up to SPF 50)” and says more research is needed to test whether SPF above 50 offers extra protection. American Academy of Pediatrics · updated 2024-08-09 medical society guidance
  • FDA has proposed (not finalized) that the maximum labeled SPF be 60+. FDA · 2021-09-24 proposed order (not final)

What points the other way

  • The AAD recommends SPF 30 or higher; it says higher SPFs block slightly more UVB rays, but no sunscreen blocks 100%. American Academy of Dermatology · updated 2025-02-11 medical society guidance

Label check

Ingredient list changed in the FDA filing
  • DailyMed, the National Library of Medicine's database of FDA label filings, keeps a new version each time a label's filing changes. US National Library of Medicine · checked weekly federal database

Not known

  • This can be a correction to the filing, not a new formula.
  • A change in the filing does not show when the product on the shelf changed; older stock may still be sold, so check the tube.
Same ingredient list as other labels
  • These labels list the same active ingredients at the same strengths and the same inactive ingredients in the same order in their FDA filings. US National Library of Medicine · checked weekly federal database

Not known

  • A matching list does not show who makes the product, or that the amounts of each inactive ingredient are the same.
Possible SPF booster listed as inactive
  • FDA's sunscreen monograph lists the active ingredients (UV filters) an over-the-counter sunscreen may use. FDA · posted 2021-09-24 (FDA has amended the monograph since) federal monograph
  • A general FDA rule requires inactive ingredients in over-the-counter drugs to be safe in the amounts used and not to interfere with the product's effectiveness; it does not address SPF boosters specifically. FDA · current federal regulation
  • This product lists the ingredient named on the card among its inactive ingredients.
  • Its status as a UV filter in the US, the EU and Australia is shown on the card. European Union · 2026-05-18 EU law
  • Hallstar, which makes butyloctyl salicylate, says it is “responsible for no more than 2 SPF units” at 5%. Hallstar (ingredient maker) · accessed 2026-10 manufacturer information
  • Vantage sells tridecyl salicylate as a “sunscreen booster” for zinc oxide and titanium dioxide. Vantage Specialty Ingredients (ingredient maker) · accessed 2026-10 manufacturer information
  • Butyloctyl and tridecyl salicylate are salicylates, the same chemical group as the approved filters octisalate and homosalate; the Cosmetic Ingredient Review assessed them together with octisalate (ethylhexyl salicylate). Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel · 2003 industry-funded expert safety panel

What points the other way

  • A 2003 Cosmetic Ingredient Review assessment that covered butyloctyl and tridecyl salicylate found these ingredients safe in cosmetics when formulated to avoid skin irritation and increased sun sensitivity (or with directions for daily sun protection). Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel · 2003 industry-funded expert safety panel
  • In 2025 the panel re-reviewed salicylic acid and 17 salicylates and found them safe as currently used in cosmetics when formulated to be non-irritating and non-sensitizing. Expert Panel for Cosmetic Ingredient Safety (CIR) · 2025 industry-funded expert safety panel
  • Hallstar says butyloctyl salicylate is “not a sunscreen active ingredient but rather a unique cosmetic ester”. Hallstar (ingredient maker) · accessed 2026-10 manufacturer information

Not known

  • We found no published study measuring whether butyloctyl salicylate or tridecyl salicylate is absorbed into the body.
  • How much of this product's SPF, if any, comes from these ingredients cannot be told from the label.
  • We found no FDA rule, guidance or warning letter about SPF boosters.
4-methylbenzylidene camphor
  • The EU banned 4-methylbenzylidene camphor in cosmetics: none could be newly sold from May 1, 2025 and none at all from May 1, 2026. European Union · 2024-04-03 EU law
  • The EU scientific committee found sufficient evidence that it may act as an endocrine disruptor, and could not set a safe concentration because data on possible genotoxicity were insufficient. EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety · 2022 EU scientific opinion

What points the other way

  • Australia's 2023 sunscreen guidelines permitted it up to 4%. Australia TGA · 2023-05 agency guideline
  • Australia's TGA published a new safety review of it in September 2026. Australia TGA · 2026-09 agency review
Bemotrizinol listed as inactive
  • FDA approved bemotrizinol as a sunscreen active ingredient (up to 6%), effective August 9, 2026. FDA · issued 2026-06-10, effective 2026-08-09 final order
  • Data submitted with the application showed it is not readily absorbed through human skin. FDA · issued 2026-06-10, effective 2026-08-09 final order
“Hypoallergenic”
  • FDA says there are no federal standards for the term “hypoallergenic”; it means whatever a company wants it to mean. FDA · accessed 2026-10 agency guidance

Check for your child

Fragrance
  • US rules let companies list all fragrance components as one word, “Fragrance”. FDA · current as of 2022-02-28 agency guidance
  • FDA says some fragrance components may cause allergic reactions or sensitivities. FDA · current as of 2022-02-28 agency guidance
  • For children with eczema, the AAD advises fragrance-free rather than “unscented” products and a fragrance-free sunscreen. American Academy of Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • The EU requires named fragrance allergens to be listed individually above 0.001% in leave-on products. European Union · 2023-07-26 EU law
  • The 2022 US law MoCRA required FDA to propose a similar fragrance allergen labeling rule by June 29, 2024. FDA (Unified Agenda) · Fall 2024 agenda federal regulatory agenda
  • Trade press reported that FDA's July 2026 regulatory agenda projected that proposal for November 2026; it had not been published as of that report. ChemLinked (trade press) · 2026-07 trade press report
  • FDA says diethyl phthalate (DEP) appears to be the only phthalate still commonly used in cosmetics, and it may be part of an ingredient listed as “fragrance”. FDA · current as of 2022-05-19 agency guidance

What points the other way

  • FDA says it does not have evidence that phthalates as used in cosmetics pose a safety risk. FDA · current as of 2022-05-19 agency guidance
Lavender or tea tree oil
  • A 2007 report described three healthy boys whose breast growth started while using products containing lavender oil (in one case also tea tree oil) and stopped when the products were stopped. New England Journal of Medicine · 2007-02-01 case reports + lab study
  • NIEHS researchers found chemicals in these oils with estrogen-like or anti-androgen activity in cell tests; an NIEHS scientist described such effects in children as very rare. NIEHS (NIH) · 2019-09 government research news

What points the other way

  • A 2021 survey study of more than 550 children reported no link between lavender or tea tree oil use and hormone effects; it came from a research center that works with natural-products brands. Franklin Health Research Center (study in Int J Pediatr Adolesc Med) · 2021-11-17 press release on a survey study (industry-linked center)

Not known

  • Case reports cannot show the oils caused it, and we found no established safe or unsafe amount.
MI or MCI preservative
  • The EU has banned methylisothiazolinone in leave-on cosmetics since February 12, 2017, because no safe level for causing skin allergy was shown. European Union · 2016-07-22, applies 2017-02-12 EU law
  • The EU has banned the MCI/MI mixture in leave-on cosmetics since 2015. European Union · 2014-09-18, applies 2015-07-16 EU law
Formaldehyde-releasing preservative
  • The EU requires products to be labeled “releases formaldehyde” when released formaldehyde exceeds 0.001%. European Union · 2022-07-08 EU law
Propyl- or butylparaben
  • The EU bans propylparaben and butylparaben in leave-on products designed for the diaper area of children under 3, because a risk for babies under 6 months could not be excluded. European Union · 2014-09-18 EU law

What points the other way

  • Outside diaper-area products for young children, EU law allows propyl- and butylparaben up to 0.14% combined, a limit set on the EU scientific committee's safety advice. European Union · 2014-09-18 EU law
  • FDA: “At this time, we do not have information showing that parabens as they are used in cosmetics have an effect on human health.” FDA · current as of 2025-11-18 agency guidance
Retinyl palmitate (vitamin A)
  • In a 2012 government study, a cream with retinyl palmitate sped up skin tumors caused by simulated sunlight in hairless mice. US National Toxicology Program · 2012-08 government research (mice)
  • EU law adopted in 2024 caps vitamin A at 0.05% (retinol equivalent) in body lotion and 0.3% in other products, and requires the label “Contains Vitamin A. Consider your daily intake before use.” European Union · 2024-04-03 EU law

What points the other way

  • The EU scientific committee found vitamin A safe in cosmetics at those limits, while noting that food and supplements alone may push the most-exposed 5% of people above the upper limit. EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety · 2022-10 EU scientific opinion

Not known

  • The study was in mice and did not draw conclusions about people; we found no FDA statement on retinyl palmitate in sunscreen.
Food-derived ingredients
  • NIH peanut allergy guidelines identify infants with severe eczema, egg allergy, or both as at highest risk of peanut allergy, and advise that, after evaluation, peanut-containing foods be introduced as early as 4 to 6 months. NIH / NIAID · 2017-01 government clinical guideline
  • A 2003 UK observational study found peanut-oil creams used on babies' rashes were associated with a higher risk of peanut allergy; the researchers said this needed confirmation and that parents should ask a doctor before changing creams. University of Bristol (ALSPAC study in NEJM) · 2003-03-11 university release on an observational study

What points the other way

  • FDA’s over-the-counter monograph lists colloidal oatmeal as a skin protectant active ingredient. FDA · 2021-09-24 federal monograph
  • The National Eczema Association lists oat among ingredients that can help soothe eczema-prone skin. National Eczema Association · accessed 2026-10 patient organization

Not known

  • The skin-exposure link comes from observational studies and is a hypothesis, not a proven cause. Refined oils may contain little protein; labels do not say.
Citrus peel oil
  • Furocoumarins, found naturally in some citrus oils such as bergamot and lime, can cause a skin reaction when the skin is then exposed to UVA light. Cutis (Janusz, Schwartz) · 2021 peer-reviewed review
  • EU law requires furocoumarins to stay below 1 mg/kg in sun protection and bronzing products. European Union · consolidated EU law

Not known

  • The label does not say whether this oil has had its furocoumarins removed.
Lanolin
  • The American Contact Dermatitis Society named lanolin its 2023 Allergen of the Year; a review published with the designation lists children and people with eczema among higher-risk groups. Cutis review (Johnson, Norman, Adler, Yu) · 2023 peer-reviewed review
  • Among children referred for patch testing in a US registry, those with eczema reacted to lanolin more often than those without eczema. Pediatric Contact Dermatitis Registry (Jacob et al.) · 2017 peer-reviewed study (referred patients)

What points the other way

  • The same review describes lanolin as a rare sensitizer in people with healthy skin. Cutis review (Johnson, Norman, Adler, Yu) · 2023 peer-reviewed review
Cocamidopropyl betaine
  • Among children referred for patch testing in a US registry, those with eczema reacted to cocamidopropyl betaine more often than those without eczema. Pediatric Contact Dermatitis Registry (Jacob et al.) · 2017 peer-reviewed study (referred patients)

What points the other way

  • The Cosmetic Ingredient Review panel concluded in 2010 that it is safe in cosmetics when formulated to be non-sensitizing, and has linked sensitization concerns to manufacturing impurities (amidoamine and DMAPA). Cosmetic Ingredient Review Expert Panel (Burnett et al.) · 2019 industry-funded expert safety panel
Propylene glycol
  • The American Contact Dermatitis Society named propylene glycol its 2018 Allergen of the Year; a systematic review calls it a weak sensitizer and an irritant. McGowan, Scheman, Jacob · 2018 peer-reviewed review

What points the other way

  • Among 6,761 people with suspected contact dermatitis patch tested in Australia (2005-2020), 21 (0.31%) reacted to propylene glycol, and 9 of them had a reaction judged relevant. Patel, Palmer, Nixon, Contact Dermatitis · 2023 peer-reviewed study (referred patients)

Eczema and psoriasis

For a child with eczema
  • The AAD: “To prevent a flare due to sunscreen, use sunscreen that contains only these active ingredients: titanium dioxide, and/or zinc oxide.” American Academy of Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • The same AAD page says the sunscreen should also be fragrance-free and broad-spectrum. American Academy of Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • The AAD says sunscreens with titanium dioxide or zinc oxide are less likely to irritate a baby's sensitive skin. American Academy of Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • The AAP's dermatology section advises mineral-based, broad-spectrum, alcohol- and fragrance-free sunscreen for sensitive areas. American Academy of Pediatrics, Section on Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • The National Eczema Association's Seal considers only physical sunscreens containing titanium, zinc and iron, and requires products to be fragrance-free. National Eczema Association · modified 2026-08-25 patient-organization certification criteria

What points the other way

  • An AAP page on eczema and sports recommends a broad-spectrum, oil-free, water-resistant sunscreen of at least SPF 15, without specifying mineral filters. American Academy of Pediatrics · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • DermNet NZ notes that many allergies to sunscreen products are caused by inactive ingredients, such as fragrances or preservatives, rather than the UV filter. DermNet (New Zealand) · 2012, updated 2024 medical reference (dated)
On the National Eczema Association's exclusion list
  • A product cannot carry the National Eczema Association's Seal if it contains any ingredient on its exclusion list, in any amount. National Eczema Association · modified 2026-08-25 patient-organization certification criteria
  • This product contains the ingredient named on the card.

What points the other way

  • The NEA says a product that works for one person may not work for another. National Eczema Association · modified 2026-08-25 patient-organization certification criteria
UV filters and skin allergy
  • A 2023 review in the journal Dermatitis names oxybenzone as the most frequently reported contact and photocontact allergen among UV filters. Ekstein and Hylwa, Dermatitis · 2023 peer-reviewed review
  • The same review found no reports of allergic or photoallergic contact dermatitis to zinc oxide or titanium dioxide. Ekstein and Hylwa, Dermatitis · 2023 peer-reviewed review
  • For people with allergy-prone skin, the AAD advises avoiding sunscreen with fragrance, parabens or oxybenzone. American Academy of Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance

What points the other way

  • Among 23,908 people referred for patch testing in North America, sunscreen was the source of an allergy for 0.9%. North American Contact Dermatitis Group (Warshaw et al.) · 2013 peer-reviewed study (referred patients)
  • Fragrance and vitamin E were among the allergens behind those sunscreen reactions, so the other ingredients matter too. North American Contact Dermatitis Group (Warshaw et al.) · 2013 peer-reviewed study (referred patients)
Alcohol
  • An AAP page on eczema in teens lists alcohols such as ethanol among ingredients that may irritate the skin, and adds that each person may react differently or not at all. American Academy of Pediatrics · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • The AAD lists alcohol among products to stop using for dry skin. American Academy of Dermatology · updated 2026-01-02 medical society guidance
For a child with psoriasis
  • The AAD says skin injuries, including sunburn, can trigger a psoriasis flare near or at the injury, about 10 to 14 days later. American Academy of Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • The AAD recommends broad-spectrum, SPF 30+, water-resistant sunscreen on uncovered skin that is free of psoriasis. American Academy of Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • The National Psoriasis Foundation recommends fragrance-free moisturizers. National Psoriasis Foundation · accessed 2026-10 patient organization
Salicylic acid
  • The National Psoriasis Foundation says the body may absorb too much salicylic acid if it is used over large areas of skin. National Psoriasis Foundation · accessed 2026-10 patient organization
  • The AAD says salicylic acid applied before phototherapy can make UVB treatment less effective. American Academy of Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance
  • EU law does not allow salicylic acid in preparations for children under 3 (the only exception is shampoos, where it is used as a preservative). European Union · 2019-11-27 EU law
Common allergens in children
  • Among 1,871 children referred for patch testing in North America (2001-2018), the most common relevant allergens were nickel (17.3%), hydroperoxides of linalool (7.8%), methylisothiazolinone (7.7%), cobalt (7.0%) and fragrance mix I (4.9%). North American Contact Dermatitis Group (Silverberg et al.) · 2022 peer-reviewed study (referred patients)

Not known

  • These figures come from children already referred for testing, so they are not population rates.

Other ingredients some parents ask about

Phenoxyethanol
  • The EU scientific committee found phenoxyethanol safe as a preservative up to 1%, including for young children. EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety · 2016-10-06 EU scientific opinion
  • In 2019 France required most leave-on products with phenoxyethanol to carry a label against use on the diaper area of children aged 3 and under; its highest administrative court annulled that decision in 2022 for lack of legal basis. France Conseil d'État (copy published by FEBEA) · 2022-12-29 court decision
PEG and other ethoxylated ingredients
  • FDA says these ingredients can carry trace 1,4-dioxane, a manufacturing byproduct it calls a potential human carcinogen. FDA · current as of 2022-03-03 agency guidance
  • New York limits 1,4-dioxane to 10 ppm in cosmetics and 1 ppm in personal care products; the state's guidance does not say whether over-the-counter sunscreens are covered. New York State DEC · accessed 2026-10 state law

What points the other way

  • FDA reports the EU committee considered trace levels of 10 ppm or less safe, and that levels have fallen over time. FDA · current as of 2022-03-03 agency guidance

Not known

  • A label cannot show whether any 1,4-dioxane is present.
BHA
  • The US National Toxicology Program lists BHA as reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen, based on animal studies; it says human data are inadequate. US National Toxicology Program · 15th edition (2021) government research
  • California lists BHA under Proposition 65 as a chemical known to cause cancer (since 1990). California OEHHA · listed 1990-01-01 state law listing

What points the other way

  • In 2026 the EU scientific committee gave its opinion that BHA is safe on skin up to 0.07% in leave-on and rinse-off cosmetics; this is advice, not yet an EU legal limit. EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (reported by CIRS) · 2026-04-16 EU scientific opinion (secondary report)
BHT
  • The EU scientific committee found BHT safe up to 0.8% in leave-on and rinse-off cosmetics (lower in mouthwash and toothpaste), a limit it set after considering concerns about BHT’s potential endocrine-disrupting properties. EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety · 2021-12-02 EU scientific opinion
Talc
  • FDA says talc can be contaminated with asbestos, a known cause of cancer when inhaled. FDA · current as of 2026-01-16 agency guidance
  • FDA proposed asbestos testing of talc cosmetics in 2024 and withdrew the proposal in November 2025, saying it will issue a new one. FDA (Federal Register) · 2025-11-28 federal notice
  • WHO's cancer agency classified talc as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A) in 2024, citing limited evidence in humans for ovarian cancer (from studies of genital use), sufficient evidence in animals and strong mechanistic evidence; it said asbestos contamination could not be ruled out in the human studies. This rates hazard, not the risk from a given product. IARC (WHO) · 2024-07-05 WHO agency classification

What points the other way

  • Health Canada concluded most uses of talc are not a concern to human health; its concerns were breathing in loose powders and genital use. Health Canada · 2021-04-22 agency assessment
Petrolatum or mineral oil
  • Germany's BfR found no health risk to be expected from highly refined (pharmaceutical-grade) mineral oils applied to the skin. Germany BfR · 2018-02-27 national agency assessment
  • The AAD recommends petroleum jelly for babies' diaper rash. American Academy of Dermatology · accessed 2026-10 medical society guidance

Not known

  • US labels do not say whether the mineral oil or petrolatum is highly refined.
Nano zinc oxide or titanium dioxide
  • In its 2019 sunscreen proposal, FDA wrote that zinc oxide stays mainly in the outer dead layers of the skin, whatever its particle size. FDA · 2019-02-26 proposed rule (superseded proposal)
  • The EU allows nano zinc oxide up to 25%, under conditions on purity and coating, but not in products that could expose the lungs by inhalation. European Union · 2016-04-21 EU law
  • The EU allows nano titanium dioxide up to 25% under similar conditions, also not in products that could expose the lungs by inhalation. European Union · 2016-07-13 EU law
  • California lists titanium dioxide under Proposition 65 only in the form of airborne, unbound particles of respirable size. California OEHHA · listed 2011-09-02 state law listing
  • Australia's TGA concluded these nanoparticles are not likely to cause harm in sunscreens. Australia TGA · 2016-08 agency review

Not known

  • US labels are not required to say whether zinc oxide or titanium dioxide is nano-sized.

Other checks we run

Less common findings, each with the rule or reference it rests on. Where a flag is our own judgment rather than an official rule, it says so.

Chemical UV filter listed among inactive ingredients

We flag this because a chemical UV filter appears among the inactive ingredients instead of being declared as a sunscreen active. It may be there as a stabilizer or at a level that adds no SPF; the label does not say which.

Source: OTC Monograph M020 / 21 CFR 201.327: sunscreen actives must be declared in Drug Facts. Reading an inactive-listed filter as undeclared is TinySafe label analysis.This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains methyldibromo glutaronitrile, banned in EU cosmetics

A preservative that causes skin allergy; the EU decided in 2003 to remove it from leave-on products and in 2007 banned it from all cosmetics, with sales ending by June 2008. The US FDA has not banned it.

Source: Commission Directive 2003/83/EC (24 Sept 2003): leave-on prohibition (text not read - date per EUR-Lex metadata and Br J Dermatol 2004;151(4):817). Commission Directive 2007/17/EC (22 March 2007): prohibited from all cosmetics (deleted from Annex VI, ref. 36; not placed on the market from 23 March 2008, not sold after 23 June 2008). Today not an authorised preservative under Annex V of Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009.
Contains IPBC

The EU does not allow it in any product for children under three except bath products, shower gels and shampoos. It is also not allowed in body lotions or body creams for any age.

Source: Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, Annex V entry 56 (iodopropynyl butylcarbamate): max 0.02% rinse-off, 0.01% leave-on, 0.0075% deodorants; not in products for children under 3 except bath products/shower gels and shampoos; not in body lotion or body cream; not in oral or lip products.
Contains an adult skincare active

We flag exfoliants, brighteners and self-tanners because we do not think they belong in baby sunscreen; that is our judgment, not a rule. The EU sets concentration limits for arbutin and the self-tanner DHA, not age limits.

Source: EU Annex III: arbutin (max 7% face cream) and alpha-arbutin (max 2% face cream, 0.5% body lotion) - Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/996; dihydroxyacetone (max 10% self-tanning) - Commission Regulation (EU) 2021/1099. US: DHA is a color additive, 21 CFR 73.2150. Classification: TinySafe judgment.This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains D4 (cyclotetrasiloxane)

The EU classifies D4 as toxic to reproduction and prohibits it in cosmetics. California's AB 496 adds it to the state cosmetics ban from Jan 1, 2027; the bill does not say whether that covers sunscreens sold as OTC drugs.

Source: EU Cosmetics Regulation Annex II entry 1388 (octamethylcyclotetrasiloxane, CAS 556-67-2), added by Commission Regulation (EU) 2019/831 - prohibited in cosmetics. REACH Annex XVII (Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/1328): D4 max 0.1% in wash-off and leave-on cosmetics. California AB 496 (2023): cyclotetrasiloxane added to the Toxic-Free Cosmetics Act ban, effective Jan 1, 2027.
Contains talc in a spray or powder

We flag talc in sprays and powders, where it can be breathed in, because of concern about asbestos contamination in talc; giving it extra weight there is our judgment. FDA proposed asbestos testing for talc cosmetics in Dec 2024 but withdrew it in Nov 2025, so no testing rule is in force.

Source: FDA proposed rule 'Testing Methods for Detecting and Identifying Asbestos in Talc-Containing Cosmetic Products', 89 FR 105490 (Dec 27, 2024), Docket FDA-2023-N-4225 - WITHDRAWN Nov 28, 2025; FDA says it will re-propose under MoCRA sec. 3505.This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains triclosan or triclocarban

Antimicrobials the FDA ruled in 2016 were not shown to be safe and effective in consumer antiseptic washes, because makers did not supply enough data. That rule covers rinse-off washes, not leave-on products.

Source: FDA final rule, Safety and Effectiveness of Consumer Antiseptics; Topical Antimicrobial Drug Products for OTC Human Use, 81 FR 61106 (Sept 6, 2016): 19 actives including triclosan and triclocarban not GRASE for consumer antiseptic washes. EU: triclosan/triclocarban restrictions updated by Regulation (EU) 2024/996.
Contains formaldehyde

A known contact allergen and carcinogen.

Source: Commission Regulation (EU) 2019/831 (22 May 2019): formaldehyde, paraformaldehyde and methylene glycol added to Annex II and deleted from Annex V entry 5. ACDS Allergen of the Year 2015: Ponten A, Bruze M. Formaldehyde. Dermatitis. 2015;26(1):3-6. doi:10.1097/DER.0000000000000075. Carcinogen: IARC Group 1.
Water-based with no listed preservative

We flag this because water-based products need some way to stop microbes growing, and no preservative is listed. Some products protect themselves in other ways, such as low water content or airless packaging, which the label may not show.

Source: 21 CFR 211.113(a) (written procedures to prevent objectionable microorganisms in non-sterile drug products); ISO 11930 and ISO 29621 (preservative efficacy / low-risk products); FDA BAM Chapter 23 (cosmetic microbiology methods). None requires a listed preservative. Flag: TinySafe judgment.This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains a phthalate

The EU bans specific phthalates in cosmetics, such as dibutyl phthalate, diisobutyl phthalate and DEHP. Not every phthalate is banned; diethyl phthalate (DEP), common in fragrance, is not.

Source: EU Cosmetics Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 Annex II: dibutyl phthalate (entry 675), diisobutyl phthalate (entry 1492, added by Regulation (EU) 2019/831); DEHP also prohibited. Diethyl phthalate not prohibited.
Contains an ingredient the EU restricts for children under 3

The EU does not allow salicylic acid in products for children under three (the 2019 rule text exempts shampoos). Boric acid is now banned in EU cosmetics for all ages, and California bans boric acid and borates from Jan 1, 2027.

Source: Salicylic acid: Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 Annex III entry 98 (amended by Regulation (EU) 2019/1966 and 2021/850) - not for preparations for children under 3 (2019/1966 text: 'except for shampoos'; survival of exception after 2021/850 not confirmed). Boric acid: prohibited, Annex II entry 1395 (2020); borate salts not individually verified. California AB 496: boric acid/borates banned from Jan 1, 2027.
Contains a PFAS ingredient (fully fluorinated)

Since 2025 several US states ban intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics. Whether those laws reach sunscreens, which the FDA regulates as drugs, differs by state.

Source: State laws banning intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics: California AB 2771, Colorado HB 22-1345, Maryland, Minnesota HF 2310 (Amara's Law), Washington HB 1047 (RCW 70A.560) - effective Jan 1, 2025 (Washington's 'intentionally added' rule from 2027); Maine (38 M.R.S. sec. 1614) and Vermont S.25 - Jan 1, 2026; Connecticut 2028. Coverage of OTC-drug sunscreens not confirmed for any state.
Contains a plant from the ragweed (Asteraceae) family

The daisy family (Compositae, also called Asteraceae) is a well-known group of plant allergens. Arnica is a stronger sensitizer than German chamomile.

Source: Wallace CE, Elston DM. Asteraceae Dermatitis: Everyday Plants With Allergenic Potential. Cutis. 2024;114(4):E18-E21. Paulsen E. Contact sensitization from Compositae-containing herbal remedies and cosmetics. Contact Dermatitis. 2002.
Contains bee-derived ingredients

This flag is driven by propolis, a documented contact allergen: in one patch-test study, about 1 to 6% of dermatitis patients reacted to it. Other bee ingredients such as beeswax and honey are included but are not the reason for the flag.

Source: Nyman GSA, Gimenez-Arnau AM, Grigaitiene J, Malinauskiene L, Paulsen E, Hagvall L. Patch Testing with Propolis of Different Geographical Origins in a Baseline Series. Acta Derm Venereol. 101(11):adv00591. doi:10.2340/actadv.v101.423 (1.3-5.8% positive in 1,470 dermatitis patients).
Contains an alkyl glucoside (such as decyl glucoside)

A plant-derived emulsifier. The American Contact Dermatitis Society named alkyl glucosides Allergen of the Year in 2017.

Source: ACDS Allergen of the Year 2017: Alfalah M, Loranger C, Sasseville D. Alkyl Glucosides. Dermatitis. 2017;28(1):3-4. doi:10.1097/DER.0000000000000234; Loranger C, Alfalah M, Ferrier Le Bouedec MC, Sasseville D. Alkyl Glucosides in Contact Dermatitis: A Systematic Review. Dermatitis. 2017;28(1):5-13. doi:10.1097/DER.0000000000000240.
Contains a sulfite

Used to keep ingredients from oxidizing. The American Contact Dermatitis Society named sulfites Allergen of the Year in 2024.

Source: ACDS Allergen of the Year 2024: Ekstein SF, Warshaw EM. Sulfites: Allergen of the Year 2024. Dermatitis. 2024;35(1):6-12. doi:10.1089/derm.2023.0154.
Contains isobornyl acrylate

An acrylate the American Contact Dermatitis Society named Allergen of the Year in 2020, mainly because of reactions to glucose sensors and insulin pumps. It is rare in sunscreens.

Source: ACDS Allergen of the Year 2020: Aerts O, Herman A, Mowitz M, Bruze M, Goossens A. Isobornyl Acrylate. Dermatitis. 2020;31(1):4-12. doi:10.1097/DER.0000000000000549.
Contains natural rubber latex

Natural rubber latex proteins can cause allergic reactions. FDA's latex labelling rule applies to medical devices, not sunscreens; we cite it only as evidence of the allergy.

Source: 21 CFR 801.437 (user labeling for medical devices containing natural rubber, 62 FR 51029, Sept 30, 1997) - a device rule, cited only as evidence that NRL proteins cause allergic reactions.
Preserved with methyl- or ethylparaben

The EU's scientific committee (SCCS) considers methyl- and ethylparaben safe at permitted levels; listed because parents ask.

Source: SCCS/1652/23, Opinion on methylparaben (adopted 14 Dec 2023, corrigendum 28 Feb 2024): safe as a preservative up to 0.4% alone (0.8% total esters). Ethylparaben: SCCS 2011 parabens opinion - methyl- and ethylparaben safe at maximum authorised concentrations.
Contains an ethanolamine (TEA/DEA/MEA)

Can form nitrosamines with certain preservatives.

Source: Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009 Annex III entries 60 (fatty acid dialkanolamides), 61 (monoalkanolamines) and 62 (trialkanolamines): not to be used with nitrosating systems; max secondary amine content; max nitrosamine 50 ug/kg; nitrite-free containers. (Free DEA's own EU status not confirmed.)
Contains silicones (non-cyclic)

Silicones are used for texture and spread; we list them for information, not as a safety flag.

Source: TinySafe informational flag. No specific CIR report cited or verified.This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Tinted with iron oxides

Mineral pigments; listed because tint changes how it looks on skin.

Source: 21 CFR 73.2250 (iron oxides, color additive for cosmetics, exempt from certification); 21 CFR 73.1200 (synthetic iron oxide, drugs).This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains a synthetic dye

Added for colour only; we list synthetic dyes for information.

Source: 21 CFR Parts 74 and 82 (certified color additives). Informational flag: TinySafe judgment.This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains SLS or SLES

Cleansing surfactants that can irritate skin, more so at higher concentrations.

Source: CIR. Final Report on the Safety Assessment of Sodium Laureth Sulfate and Ammonium Laureth Sulfate. J Am Coll Toxicol. 1983;2(5):1-34. doi:10.3109/10915818309140713.
Preserved with sodium benzoate or benzoic acid

Can cause non-allergic redness (contact urticaria) in sensitive skin.

Source: CIR. Final report on the safety assessment of Benzyl Alcohol, Benzoic Acid, and Sodium Benzoate. Int J Toxicol. 2001.
Contains plant extracts

We count plant extracts by botanical name as our own measure. A 2020 expert panel advised that baby skincare be free of sensitizing agents, but it did not single out plants.

Source: Schachner LA, et al. J Drugs Dermatol. 2020;19(8):769-776. doi:10.36849/JDD.2020.5252 ('sensitizing agent-free'; does not rank plants). Counting method: TinySafe label analysis.This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains a synthetic polymer powder (for texture)

A synthetic polymer powder added for texture and finish. The label names the ingredient; it does not show particle size or form.

Source: EU CosIng ingredient function entries. Context: EU REACH restriction on synthetic polymer microparticles, Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2055.This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains animal-derived ingredients

For families avoiding animal products.

Source: Label reading (TinySafe).This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains shea butter

Refined shea carries almost no allergenic protein; listed for families who avoid it.

Source: Chawla KK, Bencharitiwong R, Ayuso R, Grishina G, Nowak-Wegrzyn A. J Allergy Clin Immunol. 2011;127(3):680-682 (shea nut butter has no detectable IgE-binding soluble proteins). Contains coconut-derived ingredients

FDA no longer counts coconut as a tree nut for food allergen labelling. We list it for families who avoid it.

Source: FDA 'Questions and Answers Regarding Food Allergens' Edition 5 (Jan 6, 2025): coconut is no longer a FALCPA 'tree nut'. Preference flag: TinySafe judgment.This flag is our own judgment, not an official rule.
Contains cyclic silicones D5/D6

The EU limits D5 and D6 to 0.1% in leave-on cosmetics from 6 June 2027, because of environmental persistence, not skin safety.

Source: Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/1328 (16 May 2024) amending REACH Annex XVII: D5 and D6 max 0.1% in leave-on (and D6 in wash-off) cosmetics from 6 June 2027; D4/D5 already restricted in wash-off under Regulation (EU) 2018/35.

How we check the data

  • Two readers. Each label's actives, Broad Spectrum claim and ingredient list are read from the package artwork filed with the FDA. The card says whether two readers agreed, one reader read it, or only the FDA filing was available.
  • Every week. Every label is re-checked against FDA DailyMed, and the card shows the date.
  • Every month. An independent audit re-reads 300 randomly picked labels against the package. The error rate and its 95% range are shown on the Check.
  • Corrections. Anyone, parent or brand, can report an error from a product card or at angela@arwhouse.com, with a photo of the package or a link. Two readers check it within 7 days; one report alone never changes a card. Every correction is listed.

What each ingredient does

Next to every ingredient we give its main job in a sunscreen. The source is the European Commission's CosIng ingredient register, which lists the functions each cosmetic ingredient is registered for. CosIng lists every function an ingredient may serve in any product, so for the most-used ingredients two people chose the main job separately and we kept it only when they agreed. For plant extracts we name what they are used as, not a benefit they promise.

Some labels print several ingredients run together; we split those and give each its job. When a name on the label can't be identified, a cut-off word or a typo we can't resolve, we say not yet checked instead of guessing.

What a label can't tell you

This check reads labels, so it can only report what a label says. Some things that matter for a child's sunscreen never appear on one:

  • Contamination. Benzene in some aerosol sprays, bacteria or mold in a bad batch. We show it when the FDA records a recall; we cannot test products ourselves.
  • Particle size. US labels don't have to say whether zinc oxide or titanium dioxide is nano-sized.
  • Heavy metals. Mineral filters are mined; their purity is a manufacturing spec, not a label line.
  • Whether the SPF holds up. The SPF on the label is the brand's own test result. We report it as printed.
  • Amounts of inactive ingredients. Labels list them roughly from most to least, without amounts, and many brands list them alphabetically.

Propyl/butylparaben and phototoxic citrus move to 'Act on it' (EU restrictions). Synthetic beeswax no longer counts as bee-derived; an essential oil must be an oil. Ingredient jobs now come from the EU CosIng register, reviewed twice. So that what we show first follows a test anyone can check, the same for every brand.
  • v3.3 · 2026-10-22 'Contains synthetic polymer microparticles' is now 'Contains a synthetic polymer powder (for texture)'. 'Microparticles' is a term defined in the EU microplastics restriction (Regulation (EU) 2023/2055). Whether that restriction applies is a product-by-product legal judgement a label cannot settle, so the check now states the ingredient fact only. Same rule for every product; the same ingredients are matched as before.
