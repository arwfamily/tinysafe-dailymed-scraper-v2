How the Check works.

We don't score sunscreens. We read every label the FDA has on file, the printed Drug Facts and the filing behind it, and sort what we find with one written test for each group. The test is the same for every brand, including ours.

Sunnytime is checked the same way. We make a mineral sunscreen. When ours is on the FDA registry it goes through these exact rules, and what they find about it shows up like everyone else's, including the synthetic polymer powders it uses for texture.

The first three lines

Every result opens with the same three questions, in the same order, so you can compare two sunscreens at a glance.

  • Hidden UV booster. Does the label list every ingredient that absorbs UV as an active?
  • Fragrance. Does it contain fragrance, an essential oil or an EU-listed fragrance allergen?
  • Recalls. Is there an FDA recall on record for this product?

Act on it

The label hides what an ingredient does (a UV absorber that is not listed as an active), a regulator has banned or restricted the ingredient in cosmetics or in products for young children, or pediatric guidance advises against it for children.

Not broad spectrum, or below SPF 15

The label carries the FDA Skin Cancer/Skin Aging Alert, or its SPF is below 15. The FDA requires that alert on sunscreens that are not broad spectrum or are below SPF 15: they have not been shown to reduce the risk of skin cancer or early skin aging.

Source: FDA, 21 CFR 201.327.
UV booster not listed as a sunscreen active

Absorbs UV and can raise SPF while the label still reads 'mineral'.

Source: FDA OTC sunscreen monograph (only listed actives may claim SPF); TinySafe label analysis. Bemotrizinol became an FDA-approved active in June 2026 — if it appears among inactives, it is still a hidden filter.
Says fragrance-free, but lists a fragrance ingredient

The label calls the product fragrance-free (or unscented) while its ingredient list includes a fragrance or an essential oil. The two statements on the same label disagree, so check the tube.

Source: The label itself (FDA DailyMed).
Chemical UV filter listed among inactive ingredients

A chemical filter used without being declared as an active.

Source: FDA OTC monograph; FDA absorption studies (JAMA 2019, 2020).
Contains MI or MCI

Among the most common contact allergens; the EU bans MI in leave-on cosmetics.

Source: EU Regulation 2016/1198 (MI banned in leave-on); ACDS Allergen of the Year 2013.
Contains methyldibromo glutaronitrile, banned in EU cosmetics

A preservative that causes skin allergy often enough that the EU banned it from leave-on products in 2007 and from all cosmetics in 2008. It is still allowed in the US.

Source: EU Cosmetics Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, Annex II (prohibited substances); Commission Directives 2007/17/EC and 2008/42/EC.
Contains a formaldehyde-releasing preservative

Slowly releases formaldehyde, a known contact allergen.

Source: ACDS Allergen of the Year 2015 (formaldehyde); EU Annex V labelling rule for releasers.
Contains IPBC

The EU does not allow it in leave-on products for children under three.

Source: EU Cosmetics Regulation Annex V (iodopropynyl butylcarbamate entry) — verify current entry text.
Contains a retinoid

An adult skincare active; increases sun sensitivity.

Source: EU Regulation 2024/996 (vitamin A limits); AAD guidance on retinoids and sun.
Contains an adult skincare active

Exfoliants, brighteners and self-tanners aren't meant for baby skin.

Source: AAD; EU Annex III entries for arbutin and DHA.
Spray or powder form

Not recommended for children: it can be inhaled. The AAP advises against spraying sunscreen on a child's face.

Source: AAP HealthyChildren.org, Sun Safety; FDA sunscreen guidance.
Contains D4 (cyclotetrasiloxane)

Classified in the EU as toxic to reproduction; restricted in cosmetics.

Source: EU CLP classification (Repr. 2); EU REACH restriction; California AB 496.
Contains talc in a spray or powder

Talc's asbestos-contamination concern matters most when it can be inhaled; FDA proposed mandatory testing.

Source: FDA proposed rule on asbestos testing in talc-containing cosmetics (Dec 2024).
Contains triclosan or triclocarban

Antimicrobials the FDA removed from OTC antiseptic washes.

Source: FDA final rule on consumer antiseptic washes (2016).
Contains formaldehyde

A known contact allergen and carcinogen.

Source: EU Annex II (banned 2019); ACDS.
Water-based with no listed preservative

Water-containing products need a preservative system or they can grow microbes.

Source: FDA guidance on cosmetic microbiological safety; recall history for contaminated lotions.
Contains a phthalate

Endocrine-active plasticizers; restricted in the EU.

Source: EU Cosmetics Regulation Annex II.
Combined with an insect repellent

The American Academy of Pediatrics does not recommend products that combine sunscreen with an insect repellent such as DEET: sunscreen needs reapplying every two hours, repellent does not.

Source: AAP, insect and sun safety guidance.
Contains an ingredient the EU restricts for children under 3

The EU does not allow salicylic acid (except in shampoo), or boric acid and borates, in products for children under three.

Source: EU Cosmetics Regulation Annex III (salicylic acid; boric acid and borates).
Contains a PFAS ingredient (fully fluorinated)

Since 2025 several US states ban intentionally added PFAS in cosmetics. Whether those laws reach sunscreens, which the FDA regulates as drugs, differs by state.

Source: California AB 2771; Colorado, Maryland, Minnesota and Washington laws (2025); Maine and Vermont (2026).
Contains propyl- or butylparaben

The EU bans these in leave-on products for the diaper area of children under three.

Source: EU Regulation 1004/2014 (Cosmetics Regulation Annex V).
Contains a phototoxic citrus derivative

In sunlight these can cause skin reactions. The EU caps them in sunscreens at 1 mg/kg; the label does not say whether this oil is furocoumarin-free.

Source: EU Cosmetics Regulation Annex II entry 358; SCCS.
Uses chemical UV filters

Absorbed through skin; FDA has asked for more safety data on these filters before confirming them as generally safe and effective.

Source: FDA proposed rule (2019) and proposed order (2021); Matta et al., JAMA 2019/2020.

Check if your child reacts

The ingredient is on one of three named lists: the American Contact Dermatitis Society's Allergen of the Year (any year, except a year the society named a non-allergen), the EU list of fragrance allergens that must be named on labels, or the US major food allergens. Fragrance and essential oils are here because they are how those fragrance allergens get into a product.

Fragrance is the most common cause of cosmetic contact allergy.

What each ingredient does

Next to every ingredient we give its main job in a sunscreen. The source is the European Commission's CosIng ingredient register, which lists the functions each cosmetic ingredient is registered for. CosIng lists every function an ingredient may serve in any product, so for the most-used ingredients two people chose the main job separately and we kept it only when they agreed. For plant extracts we name what they are used as, not a benefit they promise.

Some labels print several ingredients run together; we split those and give each its job. When a name on the label can't be identified, a cut-off word or a typo we can't resolve, we say not yet checked instead of guessing.

What a label can't tell you

This check reads labels, so it can only report what a label says. Some things that matter for a child's sunscreen never appear on one:

  • Contamination. Benzene in some aerosol sprays, bacteria or mold in a bad batch. We show it when the FDA records a recall; we cannot test products ourselves.
  • Particle size. US labels don't have to say whether zinc oxide or titanium dioxide is nano-sized.
  • Heavy metals. Mineral filters are mined; their purity is a manufacturing spec, not a label line.
  • Whether the SPF holds up. The SPF on the label is the brand's own test result. We report it as printed.
  • Amounts of inactive ingredients. Labels list them roughly from most to least, without amounts, and many brands list them alphabetically.

Changes to these rules

  • v3.6 · 2026-10-23 The check now reads the ingredient list printed on the label, not names that appear only in the FDA filing. A filing name still counts when it is a printed ingredient under another name or says what it comes from (soy or egg lecithin, fish collagen). Strengths come from the printed Drug Facts; a strength the filing gives per volume is not converted. One filing that covers several products says so. Also: methyldibromo glutaronitrile (banned in EU cosmetics) is flagged; an SPF under 15 gets the not-broad-spectrum flag; propylene glycol esters are not propylene glycol; seed oils are not essential oils; pain-relief creams are no longer listed as sunscreens. A check of 736 labels against their printed cartons found warnings resting on names only the FDA filing had (often another scent or an older formula), strengths converted from the filing's units (13.2% shown for a printed 12%), and kits whose products were merged. Each fix is the same rule for every brand.
  • v3.5 · 2026-10-22 Three label checks added to 'Act on it': a sunscreen combined with an insect repellent (AAP), ingredients the EU restricts for children under 3 (salicylic acid, boric acid and borates), and fully fluorinated PFAS ingredients (state cosmetic bans since 2025). Each label's full text is now read for its Broad Spectrum claim and for the FDA Skin Cancer/Skin Aging Alert, which marks a sunscreen that is not broad spectrum or is below SPF 15. The EU fragrance-allergen check now covers the full list as extended in 2023 (Regulation (EU) 2023/1545), such as camphor, menthol, vanillin and citrus peel oils; when a label has only these and no fragrance, the summary says 'Contains an EU-listed fragrance allergen'. A review of what a baby-sunscreen check should catch found these on real labels and not yet covered.
  • v3.4 · 2026-10-22 Each group now has one written test. 'Check if your child reacts' is limited to three named lists (ACDS Allergen of the Year, EU labelled fragrance allergens, US major food allergens), so alkyl glucosides, sulfites and isobornyl acrylate are added; ragweed-family plants, bee-derived ingredients, oat and drying alcohol move to 'Also in it'. Propyl/butylparaben and phototoxic citrus move to 'Act on it' (EU restrictions). Synthetic beeswax no longer counts as bee-derived; an essential oil must be an oil. Ingredient jobs now come from the EU CosIng register, reviewed twice. So that what we show first follows a test anyone can check, the same for every brand.
  • v3.3 · 2026-10-22 'Contains synthetic polymer microparticles' is now 'Contains a synthetic polymer powder (for texture)'. 'Microparticles' is a term defined in the EU microplastics restriction (Regulation (EU) 2023/2055). Whether that restriction applies is a product-by-product legal judgement a label cannot settle, so the check now states the ingredient fact only. Same rule for every product; the same ingredients are matched as before.
